Acacia koa A. Gray (koa), a nitrogen-fixing tree found only in Hawaii, is the islands' most valuable native hardwood and a cornerstone of forest restoration, yet no model has existed to project how koa stands grow. Building one is hard for koa, as for many tropical forests, because most stands have no known age, which rules out the site index that conventional growth models rely on. We assembled 9,164 tree records from 158 natural and planted installations measured between 1949 and 2025 and developed a linked individual-tree growth and yield system. It joins equations for height, crown, diameter and height growth, and survival in a stand simulator that reports its uncertainty. Site productivity enters through a Biomass Yield Index, an age-free measure of the biomass a site can support. Diameter growth was about 36% faster on high than on low productivity sites, and crowding raised the chance that young plantations lost trees. Young planted koa also grew faster than naturally regenerated koa, a difference that longer records must confirm. With its key components refitted without each installation in turn, the system reproduced average survival, tree size, and basal area on 17 long-term plots. Forty years ahead, natural stands on medium and high productivity sites held 165 to 183 m³ ha⁻¹ of stem volume, or about 40 to 44 Mg C ha⁻¹ in stemwood. To our knowledge, this is the first integrated growth and yield model for koa, and it gives restoration and timber programs a quantitative basis for comparing average stand development across sites, planting densities, and thinning regimes. The age-free approach can transfer to other forests where stand ages are unknown. Longer plantation remeasurements, records of what kills trees, and links to climate are the next steps toward site-specific and climate-ready projections.
