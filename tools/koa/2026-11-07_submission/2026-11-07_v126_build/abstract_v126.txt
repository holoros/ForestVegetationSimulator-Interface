Acacia koa A. Gray (koa), a nitrogen-fixing tree found only in Hawaii, is the islands' most valuable native hardwood and a cornerstone of forest restoration, yet managers have had no integrated model to project how koa stands grow. As in many tropical forests, most koa stands have no known age, which rules out the site index that conventional growth models rely on. We assembled 9,164 tree records from 158 natural and planted installations measured between 1949 and 2025 and developed a linked individual-tree growth and yield system. Equations for height, crown, growth, and survival are joined in a simulator that reports its uncertainty. Site productivity enters through a Biomass Yield Index, an age-free measure of the biomass a site can support. Diameter growth was about 36% faster on high than on low productivity sites, and crowding raised the chance that young plantations lost trees. Young planted koa also grew faster than naturally regenerated koa, a contrast confounded with data source that longer records must confirm. With the diameter growth equation and natural mortality level refitted without each installation in turn, the system reproduced average survival, tree size, and basal area on 17 long-term plots. Forty years ahead, natural stands on medium and high productivity sites were projected to hold 165 to 183 m³ ha⁻¹ of stem volume, or about 40 to 44 Mg C ha⁻¹ in stemwood. To our knowledge, this is the first integrated growth and yield model for koa, and it gives restoration and timber programs a quantitative basis for comparing average stand development across sites, planting densities, and thinning regimes. The age-free approach may transfer to other forests where stand ages are unknown. Longer plantation remeasurements, records of what kills trees, and links to climate are the next steps toward site-specific and climate-ready projections.
